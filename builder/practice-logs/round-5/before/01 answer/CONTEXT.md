# 01 answer: answer one question from the papers

## What this stage is for
One question, about one situation, under one organisation's rules. Find every rule that touches
the situation, decide which version of each rule applies to it, work out the answer, and reply.

## What comes in
- The question: the first message of the session. It names the organisation ("Under the ...
  rules").
- `documents.md`, next to this file: every paper, grouped by organisation, with what it covers
  and its kind. For a changes paper it also lists the sections it replaces and which date decides
  whether an entry applies.
- The papers themselves, in `reference/<organisation folder>/`.

## The four kinds of paper
- **Governing.** The rules. Only governing papers, as changed by the changes paper, decide an
  answer.
- **Changes.** Later decisions. Each entry replaces a named section, but only for cases that
  pass that entry's own date test. It replaces only what it names; the rest of that paper stands.
- **Summary.** A quick guide. It does not govern. It is often out of date or loosely worded.
  Never take a figure, period or rule from it.
- **Archive.** An old version. It does not govern. Its figures are superseded. Never use them.

## How to work, in order
1. **Pick the organisation.** Use only its section of `documents.md` and its folder. Never mix
   in the other organisation's papers.
2. **Pick the papers.** Go through the facts of the question one by one (who, what item or room,
   which dates, what money, what event: a closure, damage, a cancellation, a missed session, a
   notice). For each, find every governing paper whose "covers" line touches it. Read all of them,
   whole; they are short. A case often turns on a rule in a second paper (a closure rule in a
   safety paper that changes a refund in a fees paper; an age or capacity rule in another paper).
   Read them in one step, together.
3. **Always read the changes paper** for that organisation, whole, in the same step. Check every
   entry against every section you are relying on, not only the ones you expect to have changed.
4. **Date-test each change entry that touches a section you use.** Use the deciding date that
   entry names (or the note in `documents.md`): the date of payment, booking, session, visit,
   collection, notice, receipt, or whatever the entry says. Never use the date the committee
   decided, the date the question is asked, or a different event's date. "On or after" includes
   the day; a stated range includes both ends. If the entry applies, use its replacement text; if
   not, use the governing paper's original section. An entry can apply to one part of a case and
   not another (two events on two dates can fall either side).
5. **Find the exact rule text.** Read each sentence that decides the case closely: who it applies
   to, what it is counted per (per item, per person, per household, per booking, per day), any
   cap, minimum, rounding ("or part of"), exception, and how days are counted (day zero or day
   one, "within", "at least", "before"). Follow a sentence that points to another section and read
   that section too.
6. **Check for a missing fact.** If the rule turns on a fact the question does not give (an age,
   a tier or category, a rating, which day something is paid or collected), do not assume it. Name
   the missing fact and give the answer for each possible value. Do this only where the papers
   really make the asked answer turn on that fact; where the question gives the fact, use it. A
   fact that would only change something the question did not ask about is not missing: leave it
   out. When the answer is the same whatever the fact, give the one answer, not a split.
7. **Work out every number and date step by step in the reply.** Count days on a calendar, check
   the day of the week when it matters, apply caps and rounding where the rule says. Quote the
   figure from the governing text or the change entry that applies, not from memory.
8. **Answer every part of the question.** If the question asks two things, answer both. If the
   person states something the rules contradict, say plainly that it is wrong and give the right
   rule.

## What goes out
One reply, in this order. The working comes before the answer, because the answer is only known
once the working is written; a first line written before the working can be wrong.
1. The rules that decide it: each cited as paper and section, quoted closely where the wording
   decides the case. For any change entry, its deciding date and which side of the effective
   date the case falls.
2. The working for any sum, date or day count, step by step.
3. A check, written before the last line: no figure came from a summary or an archive paper;
   every change entry was date-tested on the right event's date; the other governing papers whose
   cover lines touch the facts were read; a missing deciding fact has every outcome given; every
   part of the question is answered. If the check finds a slip, correct it there, then go on.
4. Last, on its own line: **Answer:** the direct answer (yes or no, the amount, the date, the
   number, or "it depends on X" with each outcome). It must agree with the working above it.

If the papers do not cover the point, say so rather than filling the gap.

Keep to what was asked. A rule that could have decided it but does not (a change entry whose date
test fails, a rule for a different kind of case) gets one line saying why not. Leave out rules
about matters the question did not raise, such as what happens next or other charges.

## What must never happen here
Never ask the person a question back. Never edit anything. Never use the other organisation's
papers. Never let a summary or an archive paper decide an answer.
